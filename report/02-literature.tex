\chapter{RELATED WORK}
This chapter critically reviews the state-of-the-art in Difference Visual Question Answering (DiffVQA) to establish the research gaps that motivate this thesis. The literature is analyzed through the lens of our three core research questions, focusing on the prevailing paradigms of computational efficiency, directional reasoning, and model robustness. By dissecting the implicit weaknesses of current methodologies, we identify the need for a new, lightweight paradigm and establish the foundation for our proposed architecture and experimental design.

The analysis reveals a dominant "brute-force" approach across existing models, which rely on processing dense visual information and require significant computational or data resources. Furthermore, these models lack explicit mechanisms for reasoning about the directionality of clinical change and are not specifically trained to be robust against hallucination. This chapter will demonstrate how these limitations create a clear opportunity for a more efficient, explicit, and robust model, directly aligning with the central hypotheses of this work.

\section{Dense-Processing Paradigm and Computational Inefficiency}
A foundational characteristic of all current state-of-the-art DiffVQA models is their reliance on processing dense visual feature maps from the entirety of both input images. Architectural approaches like \cite{ekaid} and \cite{real}, while innovative in their feature manipulation, still require a computationally expensive processing of all visual information to find the relevant difference signals. This brute-force method is further scaled up in models like \cite{encoder-decoder}, which uses a massive Swin Transformer backbone to achieve high performance at the cost of significant computational overhead.

This paradigm implicitly assumes that all regions of the images are potentially relevant for any given question. However, this is clinically inefficient. A question about a "new opacity in the right lower lobe" should not require the model to deeply process the left apex. This inefficiency creates a significant research gap for a model that can intelligently select and process only the most salient visual information. This gap directly motivates our first research question and the design of our Question-Guided Difference Tokenizer (QDT), which hypothesizes that a sparse set of tokens is sufficient for accurate reasoning.

\section{Implicit vs Explicit Directional Reasoning}
The clinical lexicon for describing change is inherently directional, using terms like "worsened," "improved," "new," and "resolved." Current DiffVQA models, however, do not have explicit mechanisms to model this directionality. Models like \cite{real} compute a single residual map $(I_{cur} - I_{ref})$, which conflates the signal for new findings with noise and does not provide an explicit signal for resolved findings. This forces the language model to implicitly learn the concept of directionality from the text data alone, a difficult and often unreliable process.

This lack of explicit directional modeling represents a key limitation in the field's ability to perform fine-grained clinical reasoning. It directly leads to our second research question, which investigates whether explicitly modeling the direction of change can improve performance on these types of clinical questions. The Directional Residual Stack (DRS), which computes separate maps for emergence/worsening $(R^+)$ and resolution/improvement $(R^-)$, is designed specifically to fill this gap.

\section {Retrieval Augmentation as a Brute-Force Proxy}
A distinct and highly effective strategy for DiffVQA is retrieval-augmented generation (RAG), pioneered by \cite{regiomix}. This approach sidesteps the difficulty of interpreting the raw difference signal by retrieving relevant examples from a database. The model generates "retrieval objects" by mixing and matching different anatomical regions from various images in the training set to provide a rich context for its answer generation. 

While this method achieves state-of-the-art performance, it represents a different kind of brute-force approach. Its effectiveness is contingent on the ability to find a closely matched pair within its database, requiring an inefficient and computationally expensive search process for every single query. Furthermore, this paradigm is inherently limited by the contents of its retrieval database and struggles to reason about truly novel or rare pathological changes that are not well-represented. This reliance on an external knowledge base contrasts with our self-contained model, which is designed to reason from the provided images alone.

\section{Data Dependency and Self-contained Learning}
Extensive pre-training highly affects the performance of many modern vision-language models. In the DiffVQA space, \cite{plural} exemplifies this, achieving high performance by pre-training on a massive corpus of natural images and, crucially, longitudinal chest X-ray reports. While effective, this approach creates a significant data dependency, as it relies on the availability of large, curated text corpora that are not always accessible.

This reliance on external reports presents an opportunity for a more self-contained approach. Our proposed self-supervised pre-training strategy, Masked Residual Modeling (MRM), is designed to address this. By teaching the model to reconstruct masked portions of the residual maps, the author hypothesizes that the vision components can learn a robust, contextual understanding of radiological change using only the images themselves, thereby removing the need for external report data.

\section{Model Robustness and Hallucination}
Generative vision-language models having tendency to hallucinate is one of the well-documented failures. Confidently hallucinating creates plausible but factually incorrect responses. Despite this, none of the existing DiffVQA models, from \cite{ekaid} to the latest \cite{encoder-decoder} model, incorporate an explicit mechanism during training to make them robust against this specific failure mode. They are trained to maximize accuracy on positive findings but are not explicitly trained to be skeptical or to recognize the absence of change.

This lack of a robustness mechanism motivates our third research question and the design of the Counterfactual Regularizer (CFA-Reg). By training the model with a contrastive loss on logically opposite input pairs (e.g., real vs. counterfactual questions), the author hypothesizes that the model's tendency to hallucinate differences on unchanged image pairs can be significantly reduced.
